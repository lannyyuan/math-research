# The Lighthouse Light

## Chapter 1: Snow on Christmas Eve

It was the morning of Christmas Eve, and the snow was falling on the little village by the sea.

Hazel was eight years old. She had curly brown hair, a red scarf and a green coat with two big pockets. She lived in a small white house near the harbour with her mum, her dad and her baby brother. From her bedroom window, she could see the grey sea, the boats in the harbour and, far away on the opposite side of the bay, the tall old lighthouse.

Upstairs there was Hazel's bedroom, with a bed, a pillow and a duvet, a wardrobe, a chest with a drawer for socks, a desk, a bookcase full of books, a beanbag, a rug and blue curtains. On the shelf there were her toys: dolls, a doll's pram, building blocks, marbles, dice, a chess set, a globe and a telescope. In the bathroom there was a bath, a shower, a toilet and a basin, a mirror, a toothbrush and toothpaste, soap, shampoo, a sponge, a hairbrush and a comb. Downstairs, in the living room, there was a sofa, an armchair, cushions, a carpet, a fireplace, a radiator, a television, a DVD player, a computer with a keyboard, a telephone, a vase of flowers, a plant and lots of photographs on the wall. There was a little balcony, a terrace, a garage, a lawn with a flowerbed, a hose and a sprinkler for summer, and a birdhouse in the tree.

The lighthouse was Grandad's. Every night for forty years, Grandad had climbed the stairs to the top of the tower and turned on the big lamp. In the daytime, the lamp slept. In the evening and all through the nighttime, it shone. The light turned round and round over the dark water, and every ship and every fishing boat could find home.

"Hazel! Breakfast!" Mum called from downstairs.

Hazel ran down the stairs in her pyjamas and her slippers. In the kitchen, the kettle was singing on the cooker. There was toast in the toaster, butter and jam on the table, a jug of milk, a box of cereal and a bowl of oatmeal for the baby. The baby sat in his highchair with a spoon in his fist and porridge on his nose.

At the table sat Hazel's cousin, Bud. Bud was nine. He was from America, and he was staying with them for the holiday. He had a cap on his head, glasses on his nose and a comic in his hand.

"Good morning, cousin," Bud smiled. "Look outside! It is snowing! Where I live, in the city, the snow is gray and dirty in one day."

"Grey," Hazel laughed. "Here we spell it grey."

"Gray," Bud laughed back. "And my mom says 'Mom', not 'Mum'. And I wear pants, not trousers, and sneakers, not trainers."

"Pants are your underpants!" Hazel giggled, and they both laughed so much that the baby laughed too and threw his spoon on the floor.

After that, it was a game. Bud said faucet, and Hazel said tap. Bud said cookies, and Hazel said biscuit. Bud said chips, and Hazel said crisps. Bud said truck, and Hazel said lorry. Bud said yogurt, Hazel said yoghurt. Bud said pajamas, Hazel said pyjamas. Bud said theater, Hazel said theatre. Bud said zipper, Hazel said zip. Bud said ladybug, Hazel said ladybird. Bud said Mommy and Daddy, and Hazel said Mummy and Dad. Bud said mall, apartments and candy. Hazel said shopping centre, flats and sweets.

"Same things," Hazel laughed. "Different words."

"Same world," Bud smiled. "Different colours."

"Colour with a U," Hazel smiled back.

Mum picked up the spoon and dropped it in the sink. It was a busy week. Mum had to clean the whole house: sweep with the broom, mop the tiles, brush the rug, dust the cabinets with a duster, empty the dustpan into the dustbin and the rubbish into the bin, wash the sponges, change each sheet, sew a buckle and cook. Hazel helped by standing on a stool. Now Mum looked out of the window, and her face was not happy.

"What is wrong, Mum?"

"On the radio, a man was talking about a big storm." Mum looked worried. "A blizzard. Snow and wind all night. And your dad is out on the boat."

Hazel's dad was a fisherman. Two days ago, he had gone far out to sea with his crew to catch cod and mackerel for the Christmas market. He would sail home tonight.

"Dad will see Grandad's light. He always sees the light."

Mum smiled, but it was a small, worried smile.

## Chapter 2: The Phone Call

After breakfast, Hazel and Bud put on their coats, hats, gloves and boots and ran out into the garden. The snow was soft and deep. They made a snowman with a carrot for a nose, two black stones for eyes and a stick for each arm. Bud gave the snowman his cap.

"He looks like a famous footballer," Bud laughed.

"He looks like a grumpy postman," Hazel giggled.

Then the phone rang inside the house. Ring-ring. Ring-ring.

Mum answered it. Mum's hand flew up to her mouth.

When Mum walked out, her eyes were wet. "That was the hospital. Grandad fell on the icy steps of the lighthouse this morning. He has broken his leg. The doctor says he will be fine, but he must stay in hospital tonight."

"Poor Grandad!" Hazel cried. Then she thought of something terrible. "But Mum... who will turn on the light?"

Mum shook her head. "And there is another problem. Grandad's big lightbulb broke last night. He ordered a new one from his old friend, Mrs Mallet, at her workshop in the city."

"So the lighthouse is dark," Bud whispered.

Mum did not answer.

Hazel looked at the grey sky, and at the sea, and at the dark tower across the bay. Dad was out there somewhere. And tonight there would be a storm.

"I can take the bus to the city. I can fetch the lightbulb."

"You are eight!"

"And I am nine," Bud smiled. "I can help her."

Mum looked at the baby. His cheeks were red and hot. He had a cough and a temperature. Mum could not take him out in the snow.

Hazel did not stop. "The bus stop is just at the bottom of our street. The bus drives to the city centre. We can be back by lunch."

Mum looked at the baby, then at Hazel. "Be quick," she cried. "It is only a few stops on the bus, at least." Then she gave Hazel her old phone, some money in a purse, a sandwich each, an apple each and a torch.

"Straight there and straight back. Call me from the workshop. And be careful."

"We will," Hazel promised.

## Chapter 3: The City

Brmm, brmm, the bus drove along the snowy road, past the fields and the farm, past the church and the school, and into the city.

The city was busy. There were shoppers with bags and parcels, parents with pushchairs and prams, and a child with a balloon. A policeman in a yellow jacket was standing at the crossing, waving every car, lorry and taxi on. A woman with an umbrella was walking her dog. A man on a bicycle rang his bell: ring, ring!

Hazel and Bud walked along the pavement.

"We say sidewalk," Bud laughed.

"We say pavement," Hazel laughed back.

They passed the baker's, where the baker was putting cakes and bread and pastries in the window. They passed the bookshop, the chemist's, the bank, the hairdresser, the café and the supermarket. They passed a toy shop with a dollhouse, a train set, a robot, a kite, puppets, a jigsaw and a big teddy bear in the window.

The city was full of people at work. A builder was on a ladder, a painter was painting a door, and a gardener was sweeping snow off the steps of a big hotel. A chef in a white hat was writing the menu on a board outside a restaurant, and a waiter and a waitress were setting the tables. A nurse and a dentist hurried to the hospital. A journalist and a photographer with a camera ran after a businessman and a businesswoman in grey suits. A man was selling a newspaper and a magazine. Outside the museum there was a statue of an old president on a horse, and a poster of a dinosaur: WE ARE EXTINCT! COME AND SEE OUR BONES! The dinosaurs are all dead, but each bone is still here. In the park, pigeons sat on a bench by a frozen fountain, and children were sliding down the slide in the playground, playing hopscotch, between the swings, the seesaw and the sandpit.

There was so much traffic: cars, vans, a minibus, a motorbike, a motorcycle, a scooter, a tricycle, a tram, a crane and a digger. A helicopter flew over the roofs: neeaoooowww! A big truck from the factory drove through the tunnel. Near the square, there was a church, a mosque, a synagogue and a temple, all in one street.

"Our city is like this too," Bud said. "Only taller. We have a skyscraper on every street."


In the square, there was a Christmas market. You could buy hot chocolate, cheese, candles and hats. At the bakery stall you could buy pizza, spaghetti, noodles with chopsticks, a turkey drumstick and Drinks. A fruit stall had bananas, pineapples and strawberries, and the vegetables were on a scale. The butcher had meat on his scales. There was a horse and cart, and a carriage ride, and two more carriages. At the checkout, a man paid his bill. There were old houses of red bricks and tall new buildings. There was a carousel with painted horses, and a toy train going choo, choo. There was a band with a drum, a trumpet and a guitar, and a choir singing a Christmas song. And there was a sleigh with bells.

But the sleigh was empty.

"Where is the reindeer?" a little girl cried.

A man in a red hat was running around the market. "Has anybody seen my reindeer?" he called. "She is young and small, and she has a bell on her collar! Her name is Comet!"

Nobody had seen her.

## Chapter 4: Mrs Mallet's Workshop

Mrs Mallet's workshop was next to the railway station. Inside, it smelled of oil and sawdust. There was a workbench, a vice, a drill, a hammer, a saw, a screwdriver, a spanner, a box of nails, a box of bolts and a jar of tacks. There were shelves of tins, a bottle of oil, more bottles and pipes. There was a toolbox, a ladder, a lawnmower and a broken bicycle with no tyre. On the workbench there was a plank, some sandpaper, wood shavings, a file, a penknife, scissors, a ruler, a pencil, glue, string, a peg, an iron, a plug and a piece of plastic and metal. On the wall hung an axe, a mallet and an old saddle.

Mrs Mallet was very old and very short. She had white hair in a bun, glasses low on her nose and an apron with lots of pockets. She was an engineer and a mechanic.

"So you are Hazel," she smiled. "Your grandad talks about you every day. And who is this?"

"I am Bud. I'm her cousin. From America."

"Welcome!" She took a large box from a high shelf. Inside, wrapped in paper and cotton, was the lightbulb. It was as big as a melon and as clear as ice.

"Be very, very careful. If this should break, there is not another one in the whole country."

Hazel slid the box into her backpack. It was heavy.

Then Mrs Mallet opened a cupboard and took out something else. It was a little metal robot, as tall as a cereal box. It had a square head, round eyes, a red button on its chest and wheels instead of feet. One arm was broken.

"This is Bolts. Robots are my hobby. I made him when I was a girl. He is very old now, like me. He can walk and talk, and his eyes light up like a torch. But his battery does not last long. Take him with you. A long journey is better with a friend."

She put in a new battery and fixed his arm with a tiny screw. She pressed the red button.

Beep. Bleep. Ping!

The robot's eyes lit up, yellow and bright.

"Hello. Happy to meet you," Bolts beeped. "I am Bolts. I like nuts. Not the kind you eat. My little body does five things: seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting and touching. Taste is not useful for robots." 

Bud laughed. "A robot with jokes!"

Bolts had a little screen on his chest. "Do you want to see my photographs?" he asked. Pictures popped up, one by one: Mrs Mallet as a little girl with blonde hair and a red ribbon, at a school desk; Mrs Mallet at her wedding, a beautiful bride in white, with the bridegroom in a suit and a tie with a rose in his buttonhole, a bridesmaid with a bonnet, and lots of guests; Mrs Mallet with her husband and their baby son and daughter; Mrs Mallet as a grandmother with her grandson and granddaughter; and Mrs Mallet with her pet canary in a birdcage.

"She was a clever girl," Bolts beeped. "Very smart. She could fix anything."

"I still can," Mrs Mallet smiled.

## Chapter 5: The Station

Hazel called Mum on the phone. "We have the lightbulb!"

But at the bus stop, there was a sign: NO BUSES TODAY. SNOW.

The wind was stronger now.

"The train!" Bud cried. "We can take the train to the village station!"

They ran into the station. The station had a big clock, a ticket office, a waiting room, benches and a café. On the platform, there was a trolley, luggage and a suitcase. But there were no trains.

"No trains today." The man in the ticket office shook his head. "The signals are frozen and there is snow on the line. The railway is closed."

"Then we have to walk."

"How far is it?" Bud asked.

"Ten miles."

"That is far."

"Calculating," Bolts beeped. "That is very far."

Hazel called Mum again. But now there was no signal. The phone said NO SERVICE.

Hazel thought of Grandad in his hospital bed, and of Dad out on the dark sea.

"I am going."

Bud pulled his hat down over his ears. "Then I am going too."

"And I am coming," Bolts beeped. "Somebody has to carry the jokes."

They walked out of the station, to the last platform, past the buffers, and onto the old railway path.

And behind them, in the snow, something was following.

Jingle. Jingle.

## Chapter 6: The Little Reindeer

The old railway path ran straight between two long hedges. On one side there was a canal, frozen and white. A barge was stuck in the ice, with a little chimney and a line of washing with pegs on its roof. On the other side there were fields, and a fence, and a signpost that pointed to THE COUNTRYSIDE, THE FARM and THE SEA.

Jingle. Jingle.

Hazel turned round.

Behind them, in the snow, stood a small brown reindeer with big dark eyes, two little horns and a red collar with a silver bell.

"Comet!" Bud cried. "It's the missing reindeer from the market!"

The reindeer was shaking. There was snow on her back and ice on her fur. She was very young, not much bigger than a foal or a calf.

Hazel walked to her very slowly, with her hand out. "Hello, Comet," she whispered. "Are you lost too?"

Comet pushed her soft nose into Hazel's hand. Her nose was cold and wet.

Bud shook his head. "We can't take her back to the city. It's too far, and the storm is coming."

"Then she can come with us," Hazel decided. "We can take her home tomorrow."

Comet licked Hazel's glove. Jingle, went the bell. It sounded like yes.

"New friend detected," Bolts beeped. "Species: reindeer. Favourite food: unknown. Jokes: none yet."

So now there were four of them: a girl, a boy, a robot and a reindeer.

Bud began a game.

"I spy with my little eye," he began, "something beginning with... S."

"Snow," Hazel guessed.

"No."

"Sky?"

"No."

"Snowflake? Sheep? Signpost? Scarf? Stick? Stone?"

"No! Snail!" Bud pointed. On a wall, under a tiny roof of snow, there was a sleeping snail.

Hazel laughed. "My turn. I spy something beginning with... W."

"Wall. Wind. Windmill. White. Wood. Wheel. Water."

"No. Wolf."

Bud stopped. "Where?"

Hazel giggled. "Only joking."

To keep walking, they talked about school.

"In my school, I am in Class Three," Hazel said. "Our classroom has a big board, chalk, an easel, a computer and a fish tank. My teacher is Miss Pepper. Every day we have lessons: reading and writing, the alphabet and letters and words, maths with numbers and sums, science, history, geography, art and music. I have a pencil case with pens, pencils, crayons, felt-tips, a rubber, a ruler, glue and scissors, and a notebook, a workbook and a diary. I have my own locker. Art is my best lesson. I can draw and paint with paints and paintbrushes."

"I love sport," Bud said. "I am in the school team for basketball and baseball. In America, football is a different game."

"I play football and tennis and badminton," Hazel said. "And in summer we have a sports day, with a race and a prize. Last year I was the fastest runner in my class."

They counted every sport they knew: golf, hockey, rugby, cricket, volleyball, archery, judo, karate, gymnastics, cycling, jogging, swimming, skiing, snowboarding, ice-skating, windsurfing, rowing, sailing and riding. Bud's uncle was a water-skier. Hazel's auntie liked hang-gliding.

"I would like to try skiing on this hill," Bud said. "With a snowboard. Or a sledge."

"You can try a snowball," Hazel laughed, and she threw one at him.

## Chapter 7: The Farm

By the afternoon, the snow was coming down so fast that they could not see the path. The wind began to blow hard. Hazel's fingers were frozen inside her gloves, and her toes were frozen inside each boot.

Then, through the snow, they saw a light. It was a farmhouse with a red door and smoke coming out of the chimney. Beside it there was a big barn, a cowshed, a stable, a pigsty, a hen house, a greenhouse, a shed and a tall haystack covered in snow.

A sheepdog ran out barking. Woof! Woof!

Behind the dog walked the farmer. She was a big woman in a woolly hat and a fleece.

"What are you children doing out in this weather? And with a reindeer!"

Hazel talked and talked.

The farmer listened. Then her big warm hand was on Hazel's shoulder. "Come inside. You need hot soup before you walk one more step."

The farmhouse kitchen was warm and full of sweet smells. There was a big old stove, a fridge with a drawing on it, a microwave, a dishwasher, a sink and a tap that dripped. On the shelves there were jars of honey, jam and pickles, a cookbook, a teapot, mugs, cups and saucers. Saucepans hung on hooks over the stove, next to a colander, a sieve, a grater and a whisk. There was a mixer on the counter, a bowl of flour and sugar, and a tray of fresh biscuits.

The farmer cooked a pan of soup on the stove. It was vegetable soup, with potatoes, carrots, an onion, leeks, peas, beans, cabbage and celery. She cut bread and butter. She gave them cheese, ham, an omelette and a jug of milk. She put a plate of hay and apples on the floor for Comet.

"In America we say omelet," Bud laughed, with his mouth full.

"Eat your omelet, then," the farmer laughed.

After lunch, they helped her with the animals.

In the barn, a cow and a bull said moo, and two calves said moo too. In the cowshed, a calf began to drink milk from its mother. In the pigsty, the pigs and the piglets said oink. In the field, the sheep said baa, and the sheepdog brought them into the barn, with the lambs running behind. In the stable, the horse said neigh, the pony stamped its foot, and the donkey said hee haw. A goat ate the straw. A lamb was asleep next to its mother. In a hutch, a rabbit was eating cabbage, and in a kennel, a puppy went woof. A kitten went meow. Bud counted the cows, the ducks, the goats, the chicks and all the birds. There were too many to count. The hens said cluck, a chicken sat on her eggs, and a chick said cheep. The cockerel and the rooster on the gate cried cock-a-doodle-doo, even though it was not morning. A duck and her ducklings said quack. A goose, the geese and a little gosling went hissssss, and the turkeys said gobble, gobble.

"I didn't know farms were so noisy!" Bud laughed.

"Noise detected," Bolts beeped. "Very, very much noise."

In the hayloft, a cat and three kittens were asleep in the hay. A mouse ran across the floor, and a rat ran after it.

There was a tractor in the barn with a trailer and a plough. There was a wheelbarrow, a rake, a hoe, a spade, a shovel, a trowel, a watering can and a scarecrow with a straw hat. Outside, in the orchard, the apple trees and pear trees and plum trees were covered in snow.

When the animals were safe, the farmer looked out at the snow. "My tractor can't drive on the road in this. The snow is too deep. But I can give you something."

She gave Hazel a big, warm blanket, a bag of sandwiches, a flask of tea, a box of matches and a long rope. She gave Bud a woolly hat with a pompom. She gave Comet a carrot. And she gave Bolts a spare battery from the tractor's toolbox.

"The stream will lead you through the wood. Then climb over the hill. Your grandad is a good man."

## Chapter 8: The Hedgehog

The trees were tall and black, and their branches were heavy with snow. Sometimes a branch broke with a crack, and a pile of snow fell down: whoosh!

Bolts's eyes lit up the path like two little torches.

Under an old oak tree, there was a pile of leaves. The pile was moving.

"Something is in there," Bud whispered.

Out of the leaves rolled a small, spiky, brown ball. It uncurled. It had a pointed nose, two tiny black eyes and four little paws.

"A hedgehog!" Hazel cried.

"Who woke me up?" it squeaked. "It is winter. I am supposed to be asleep until spring. A branch fell on my nest, and now I am awake, cold and very, very grumpy."

"I'm sorry," Hazel whispered. "What is your name?"

"Pebbles," the hedgehog sniffed. "Because I am small and fat and round and grey, like a pebble on the beach."

Bud looked at the snow. "You can't stay out here. You'll freeze."

Hazel looked at Bud's new woolly hat. Bud looked at Hazel. He sighed, took off the hat and held it out.

Pebbles climbed in, turned round three times and curled up. "Not bad," he mumbled. "A little itchy. But not bad."

Bud dropped the hat with the hedgehog into his jacket pocket, with just the pointed nose sticking out.

A badger looked out of its den and then hid again. A fox ran across the path. A deer and a hare watched them from behind a bush. A squirrel looked down from a branch. Up in a tree, an owl went hoot-hoot, and a little owlet called back. Far away, on the hill, something went hoooowl.

Pebbles poked his nose out of the pocket. "That," he said, "is a wolf."

There were smaller things too, sleeping in the wood for the winter. Bolts shone his light on them as they passed: a worm and a slug under a log, a beetle and a woodlouse under a stone, a spider in a cobweb, ants in a hole in a tree, a moth on the bark and a caterpillar asleep in a chrysalis. There were nuts and an acorn on the ground, a mushroom by a root, a fern under the snow and a bird's nest in a bush.

"The forest is full of sleeping things," Hazel whispered.

"Everybody sleeps in winter," Pebbles yawned. "Except silly children with lightbulbs."

## Chapter 9: The Castle

At the top of the wood, on a rocky hill, stood an old castle. In summer, hikers come here. It was a ruin now. The roof was gone, and there were holes in the walls. There were old logs and sticks from a bonfire. But one tower still had a dry room.

"We can rest here for a while," Hazel whispered. Her legs were so tired.

They sat on the floor, wrapped in the farmer's blanket. Comet lay down next to them. Bud poured hot tea from the flask, and they ate the farmer's sandwiches.

"Tell us a story, Bolts!" Hazel smiled.

Bolts showed them a story on his screen.

"Once upon a time," he began, "in this castle, there lived a king with a gold crown, a queen with a silver tiara, a prince and a princess. They had a palace full of servants, a servant for every room, soldiers and knights. Each knight had armor, a sword, a shield, a bow, an arrow and lots more arrows. One day a dragon flew from the mountains. It had a wing on each side, a long tail and fire in its mouth. It was big and ugly, but its eyes were sad. ROAR!"

"Every soldier was scared. Every knight was scared. Even the king was scared. But the princess was not scared. She ran to a wise old wizard and a kind witch. The wizard had a magic wand. The witch had a cauldron and a broomstick. They made a spell. Then the princess climbed up a giant beanstalk..."

"That is a different story," Bud laughed.

"I am mixing my files," Bolts admitted. "It is an old memory. ...The princess climbed up the beanstalk and met a giant, a genie, an elf, a fairy, a unicorn and a mermaid—"

"A mermaid on a beanstalk?" Hazel laughed.

"—and they all asked the dragon one question: why are you so angry?"

"And?" Pebbles squeaked.

"The dragon said: I am not angry. I am lonely. Nobody comes to play with me, because everybody is scared of me. So the princess invited the dragon to the palace for a party. There was cake and music, and the dragon lit the candles with his fire. And they all lived happily ever after. The end."

Bud took his comic out of his pocket. "That was better than my comic. In my comic, the superhero will fight the monster. KAPOW! WHAM! BOOM!"

"Fighting is not always the best way," Bolts beeped.

## Chapter 10: Being Scared

Outside, the storm was getting worse. It was nearly dark.

"Can I tell you something?" Hazel said quietly. "I am scared of the dark. I always have been. At home, I sleep with my lamp on."

Bud pushed his glasses up his nose. "I'm scared of being so far from home. My mom and dad are in America. It's a long way. Sometimes at night, I miss them so much."

"I am scared of the wolf," Pebbles said in a tiny voice. "And of owls. And of foxes. And of cars. And of most things, really."

Comet's head was on Hazel's knee. Jingle. Maybe she was scared of being alone.

Bolts was quiet. Then he said, "I am scared that my battery will run out. When my battery runs out, I go to sleep. And sometimes, nobody wakes me up for years. I slept in Mrs Mallet's cupboard for twenty years."

"Twenty years!" Hazel said. "That is so sad."

"It was very boring," Bolts agreed. His screen could show every feeling: happy, sad, bored, calm, confused, confident, excited, surprised, hungry, thirsty, greedy, lazy, naughty, polite, friendly, helpful, clumsy and graceful. Now it showed a sad face.

Then Hazel said, "It's funny. When I say it out loud, the scared feeling gets a bit smaller."

"Like a balloon," Bud smiled, "when you let the air out."

"We are all scared of something," Bolts beeped. "But we are together. Together is less scary."

Hazel switched on Mum's torch and pointed it at the ceiling of the tower. There were cobwebs, and a bat hanging upside down, fast asleep. The light made a big round circle on the stone.

Hazel stood up. "Come on. We have to keep going. The lighthouse is waiting."

## Chapter 11: The Winter Fairground

On the other side of the hill, in a valley, there was a fairground. In summer it was full of people and music and lights. But now, in winter, it was closed.

"It's a shortcut," Hazel smiled. "Grandad's words: walk through the fairground and down to the sea."

They walked in through the big gate. There was the helter-skelter, white with snow. There was the big wheel, still and dark. There were the dodgems, the roundabout, the rollercoaster and the bouncy castle. There was a hoop-la stall with a sign that said WIN A PRIZE!, and a stall with a target, darts and a big teddy bear. There was a popcorn van and a candyfloss van, both locked.

At the back of the fairground there was a big striped circus tent.

Inside the tent, it was dry. There were benches, a ring of sawdust and posters. One poster showed the ringmaster in a red coat and a tall black hat. One showed the clown with a red nose and big shoes. One showed an acrobat on the trapeze and two acrobats on the tightrope, where balance is everything. One showed a juggler with balls and clubs. One showed a dancer, a singer and the band: a drum, drums, a trumpet, a tambourine, a xylophone, a guitar and a piano. One showed a ballerina in a pink dress doing ballet on the back of a white horse.

"In summer they have a concert here," Hazel whispered. "And a puppet show, and a magic show, and dancers and singers. Grandad brings me every year."

Bolts found a box in the corner. Inside there were masks, a microphone, a recorder, a tambourine and an old film camera. The masks were a ghost, a vampire, a beast and a hero. There was a badge that said SCORE: 100, a racket, a hoop, roller skates, a trampoline and a poster for a carnival, a fair with a chairlift, and a cinema show. There were pictures of children dancing, jumping and diving.

Bud picked up the microphone. "Ladies and gentlemen!" he shouted in a big voice. "Welcome to the greatest show in the world! Starring: Hazel, the brave lightbulb girl! Bolts, the robot with jokes! Comet, the reindeer with a bell! And Pebbles, the grumpy hedgehog!"

Pebbles poked his nose out of the hat. "I am not grumpy," he squeaked grumpily.

Hazel laughed so much that she nearly forgot to be tired.

Before they left, Hazel took a long ladder from the back of the tent. "We might need this. There is a stream at the bottom of the valley, and the bridge is old."

## Chapter 12: The Ice

Grandad was right. At the bottom of the valley, there was a wide stream, and it was frozen. The old wooden bridge had fallen into the ice.

"We can cross on the ice," Bud smiled. "Look, it's thick."

"Never walk on ice!" Hazel cried. "Grandad told me!"

"There's no other way." And before anybody could stop him, he stepped onto the ice. One step. Two steps. Three steps.

Crack!

The ice broke, and Bud fell through into the black, freezing water.

"BUD!" Hazel screamed.

Bud splashed and kicked. Splish, splash! His glasses fell off. The water was so cold that he could not breathe. He grabbed the edge of the ice, but it broke again.

Hazel did not think. She pushed the ladder flat across the ice, like a bridge, until the top of it reached Bud. "Hold the ladder!" she shouted.

Bud held on. Hazel tied the farmer's rope to the ladder and threw the rope to Comet. "Comet! Pull!"

The little reindeer took the rope in her teeth. She began to dig her hooves into the snow and pulled. Hazel pulled. Bolts pulled with his one good arm.

Slowly, slowly, the ladder slid back across the ice, with Bud on it. Then he was out, on the snow, wet and shaking.

"My glasses," he coughed.

Bolts rolled to the edge of the hole and fished them out with his hand. "Glasses retrieved," he beeped.

Hazel wrapped Bud in the blanket. His lips were blue. His fingers were white. "We need to get you warm. Fast."

## Chapter 13: The Caravan

Near the stream, in a field, there was a campsite. In summer there were tents, a camper and caravans. Now there was just one old caravan, covered in snow.

The door was not locked. Inside, there was a little table, a curtain, a cushion, a sofa, a stove, a sink, two bunk beds with pillows and a duvet, and a cupboard full of things.

Hazel lit the little stove with the farmer's matches. Bud took off his wet clothes. In the cupboard, Hazel found a dry jumper, a sweatshirt, a pair of jeans, some woolly socks and a big towel.

"In America, we say sweater, not jumper," Bud began, but his teeth began to chatter.

"Just put it on!"

Soon the caravan was warm. Bud stopped shaking. Hazel made hot chocolate in a saucepan with milk from the cupboard. Pebbles sat by the stove, warming his paws. Comet lay down by the door.

Bud looked at Hazel. "You saved my life."

"Comet did. And Bolts."

"And the ladder," Pebbles squeaked.

Bud looked at his hands. "That was a stupid thing to do. I'm sorry."

"It's okay. Everybody makes mistakes."

The stove went tick, tick, and they talked about their families.

Bud talked about his family in America. "My mom is a doctor, and my dad is a teacher. I have a big sister who is a teenager. She has blond hair, a skateboard, a bike and a scooter, and she listens to music all day. My mom has auburn hair. My grandpa is thin and nearly blind, but he is the oldest man alive, and his stories are very exciting. I have twin brothers. Each one is a toddler. And we have pets: a goldfish, a hamster in a cage and a budgerigar called Captain."

He talked about his city: the skyscrapers, the apartments, the tram and the taxis, the busy streets and the airport, where a plane goes up and down the runway every minute.

Hazel talked about her family: her mum, who works in the library as a librarian; her dad, the fisherman; her baby brother; and Grandad and Granny. "Grandad is my mum's father," she explained. "So he is my grandfather, and I am his granddaughter. Granny made the scarf I am wearing. She knitted it with red wool."

"And what about you, Bolts?" Bud asked. "Do you have a family?"

Bolts's eyes flickered. "Mrs Mallet. She is my family. She made me when she was a little girl. She is very old now. Sometimes I worry about her."

"Hedgehogs don't have families," Pebbles sniffed. "We live alone."

"You have us now," Hazel smiled.

Pebbles did not say anything. But he moved a little closer to Hazel's hand.

"Siblings, cousins, grandparents," Bud counted. "Families are funny. I call mine Grandma and Grandpa. My friend calls his Gran and Gramps. Another friend calls his Nan, Nana and Grandpop. In some places, it's Ma and Pa, or Mama and Papa. My aunt is Mom's sister. I am her nephew, and my sister is her niece. And all of us are grandchildren."

"Or twins," Hazel smiled. "Or a husband and wife."

"Grown-ups always ask: what do you want to be?" Bud asked.

"A vet," Hazel smiled. "Or an artist, or a writer, or an explorer, or a climber."

"A pilot," Bud smiled. "Or an athlete, or a scientist, or a detective, or a judge, or a lawyer. Or a musician. My mom says an actor or an actress has fun every day."

"I want to be an optician," Pebbles squeaked. "For hedgehogs. We can't see very well."

"When I am an adult," Bolts beeped, "I want to be a babysitter. Or a nanny. Or one of the mechanics at Mrs Mallet's workshop."

## Chapter 14: The Dream

Hazel closed her eyes, just for a minute. And she had a dream.

In the dream it was summer. It was a hot, sunny day in July or August, and she was at the seaside. The sand was warm. Dad was there, and Mum, and the baby in his sunhat. Hazel had her bucket and spade, and she was building a sandcastle, and then more sandcastles. She found a shell, a clam and a crayfish in a rock pool, and a sunstar, which is a starfish with lots of arms.

There were kids, a kid in a swimsuit, and a lady in a deckchair with suncream on her nose. There was a man selling ice cream. There were people with sunglasses and sunblock, towels and umbrellas, beach balls and kites. A family was having a picnic and a barbecue: a sausage, a burger, a hamburger, salad and watermelon. There was a lifeguard on a tall chair, and a dog chasing a ball into the waves.

On the rocks, Hazel found shells, pebbles, seaweed, a crab, a starfish, a shrimp and a tiny lobster. Out at sea, there was a yacht, a sailboat, a kayak, a canoe, a rowboat with an oar, a sailor in a submarine, a pirate ship, a speedboat, a motorboat, a ferry and a big tanker. A seagull went squawk over her head. A seal swam by and waved its flipper.

Then the dream changed. Hazel was under the sea, with goggles and flippers, swimming like a diver. There was a coral reef full of fish: goldfish and carp, cod and mackerel, a seahorse, a stingray, an octopus, a turtle and a shark. A dolphin swam by, then a whale, then an orca. She swam into a cave, through the estuary of a river, and past a tepee on the beach. "Ooh," she thought. "Woooh!" On the seabed there was an old shipwreck with a treasure chest full of gold coins and diamonds.

Then the dream changed again. She was at the zoo with Grandad. They saw an elephant with a long trunk, a giraffe, a zebra with black and white stripes, a lion with a big mane, two lions with their cubs, a tiger and a tiger cub, a leopard with spots, a cheetah and a jaguar. They saw a monkey, a gorilla and a chimpanzee, a panda and a koala, a kangaroo with her joey in her pouch, a camel, a bison, an antelope, a hippopotamus (Grandad calls it a hippo), a rhinoceros (Grandad calls it a rhino), a crocodile, an alligator, a snake, a lizard, a scorpion and a big hairy tarantula. They saw a penguin, a parrot, a parakeet, a flamingo, a pelican, an eagle with a hooked beak, an ostrich and a peacock with beautiful feathers. In the aquarium, an otter and a beaver were swimming.

Then she was in a seaplane, then a biplane, then a glider with a parachute, flying over an island, an iceberg, an igloo, the ocean, a river, a lake, a waterfall, a volcano, a mountain, a jungle, a desert and the Savanna. Then she was in a rocket. She could fly into space. She was an astronaut, looking down at the planet Earth. She saw the moon and the stars and a comet with a long tail. An alien in a little spaceship and two green spacemen waved at her.

"Hazel," the alien whispered. "Hazel. Wake up."

"Wake up!" It was Bud, shaking her shoulder. "Bolts thinks the storm is getting worse. We must walk on."

## Chapter 15: The Cliff Path

It was night now, and the storm was terrible. The wind was so strong that Hazel could hardly stand. The snow flew sideways. It was a stormy, windy, wild night. Sometimes there was rain, and sometimes there was hail, hard and icy, that stung their faces. Far out over the sea, there was lightning, and a long rumble.

The path ran along the top of the cliffs. Far below were the rocks and the huge black waves.

"Stay away from the edge!" Hazel shouted.

They walked in a line: Hazel first, with the torch; then Bud; then Comet; and Bolts last, rolling slow through the snow. Pebbles was in Bud's pocket.

And then, in a big gust of wind, Bud slipped. He did not fall over the cliff, but the hat with Pebbles fell out of his pocket and rolled away into the dark.

"Pebbles!" Bud cried.

They looked everywhere. Hazel shone the torch. Bolts shone his eyes. But there was only snow, and dark, and wind.

Then they heard it: a tiny, angry squeak.

The hat was stuck in a bush right at the edge of the cliff. Pebbles was holding on with his little paws.

"Don't move!" Hazel shouted. She lay down flat on the snow and began to crawl to the edge. Below her, the waves crashed on the rocks. She stretched out her arm, further, further... and her fingers closed around the hat.

She pulled it back, and crawled away from the edge, and held the hat against her chest.

"You came back for me," Pebbles whispered.

"Of course we came back. You're our friend."

For the first time, Pebbles did not say anything grumpy at all.

## Chapter 16: The Lighthouse

At last, through the storm, they saw it: the lighthouse, tall and dark, on its rock across the bay. Below it, the harbour lights were on, but the sea beyond was black.

She took Grandad's spare key from under the doormat and opened the heavy front door.

Inside, there was a little round room with Grandad's armchair, his radio, his kettle, his mug, a calendar on the wall and a photo of Hazel on the shelf. On the calendar Grandad had written in pen the days of Dad's trip: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday and Sunday. December was nearly over. Next to it was a map of the bay with all the boats' names. Under the calendar there was Grandad's weather diary for every week, every month and every weekend: frost and fog in January and February, a frosty, foggy mist in March, rainy days and a rainbow in April, dew in May, Hazel's birthday, breezy days in June, clouds in September, a cloud like a whale in October and a cloudy, windy November. The four Seasons: Spring, Summer, Autumn and Winter. Tick-tock, went the old clock.

Then there was the hall, a mat and the stairs. Climbing was not easy. Hazel counted as she climbed: "Six, seven, eight... eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty..." After a quarter of the way, her legs hurt. After half, she could hardly breathe. At the third window, she looked down. Her backpack was heavy. Bud climbed behind her with Pebbles. Comet waited at the bottom. Bolts could not climb stairs, so Bud carried him under his arm.

At the top, behind the railings, was the lamp room. In the middle stood the great lamp, with its big glass lens like a giant eye, and in the middle of the lens was the empty socket for the bulb.

Very carefully, Hazel took the box out of her backpack. She opened it. The lightbulb was safe. Not a single crack.

She screwed it into the socket. Turn, turn, turn. Click.

Then she pressed the big switch on the wall.

Nothing happened.

Hazel pressed it again. Nothing.

"The storm has cut the electricity," Bolts beeped. "There is no power."

"There's a generator!" Hazel cried. "Downstairs!"

They ran down the stairs. In a little room at the bottom, there was an old generator with an engine. Hazel pulled the cord. Nothing. She pulled again. The engine coughed, and stopped.

"Battery is flat," Bolts beeped. "It needs a battery to start."

Hazel looked at the spare battery from the farmer. She put it in. She pulled the cord. The engine coughed, and coughed, and stopped. "It's not strong enough." She wanted to cry.

Bolts rolled forward.

"Use my battery. It is a special battery. Mrs Mallet made it. It is very strong."

"But Bolts... If we take your battery, you will go to sleep."

"I know. But the boats need the light. Your dad needs the light." His yellow eyes looked at her. "And you will wake me up again. Won't you?"

Hazel's eyes filled with tears. "I promise."

Bud opened the little door in Bolts's back and took out the battery. Bolts's eyes turned dim. "Goodnight, friends," he whispered. And then he was quiet.

Hazel pushed the battery into the generator. "Three, two, one, zero," Bud counted.

She pulled the cord.

The engine roared into life.

## Chapter 17: The Light

Far above them, at the top of the tower, the great lamp lit up.

The light shone out through the storm, white and bright and strong. It turned round and round over the black water, one flash every five seconds.

Hazel and Bud ran outside with Comet and Pebbles.

At first, there was nothing out at sea but dark and waves.

Then, far away, a small light answered. Then another. And another.

One by one, the boats sailed in out of the storm, following the beam of the lighthouse into the harbour. A fishing boat. Another fishing boat. The ferry, with all its windows bright. A sailing boat with a broken mast. And at last, a little blue boat with a white stripe, a flag on a pole, a net, bait in a barrel and a paddle on the deck. Toot, toot! went its whistle.

"That's Dad's boat!" Hazel screamed. "That's Dad!"

Down at the harbour, there were lights and people everywhere. There was an ambulance and a fire engine. There was a policeman and a policewoman, and a firefighter in a helmet. And there, running along the sea wall with the baby in her arms, was Mum.

When Dad climbed off his boat, he ran up the path to the lighthouse, and he picked Hazel up and held her tight, and he did not let go.

"I saw the light," he whispered into her hair. "I was lost in the worst storm I know. I could not see anything. And then I saw Grandad's light."

"It wasn't Grandad's light tonight," Bud smiled. "It was Hazel's."

## Chapter 18: Christmas Morning

On Christmas morning, the storm was over. The sky was blue, and the sun was shining on the snow.

Grandad was home from the hospital in a wheelchair, with his leg in a white cast and a pair of crutches. Grandad was bald, with a big white beard. He sat in his armchair by the fire, with a blanket on his knees, and he listened to the whole story twice. "You are the bravest granddaughter in the world," he smiled.

"How did it happen, Grandad?" Bud asked.

"An accident," Grandad said. "The steps were icy, and I fell. At the hospital, the receptionist sent me to a doctor, and the doctor took an x-ray. My leg was broken in two places. A nurse with a syringe made a plaster cast and a bandage, gave me medicine and pills, and took my temperature with a thermometer. The man in the next bed had a headache, an earache, a toothache and a cold. He did nothing but sneeze all night. I gave him all my tissues and my handkerchief."

Then it was present time.

Under the Christmas tree there were presents in all shapes: a cube, a cone, a pyramid, an oval, a rectangle, a triangle and a hexagon. On the tree there were little things shaped like a star, a heart, a diamond, a crescent moon, a semicircle and a pentagon. Each present had a ribbon and a card. There were Christmas cards and a letter, each in an envelope with a stamp. There was even a postcard from Bud's family in America, with a picture of London on it, because they love to travel.

Hazel opened her presents. She got a cardigan, a T-shirt, a shirt, a skirt, shorts, tights, a nightdress, a belt, new sandals for summer, a necklace with beads, earrings, and a barrette for her hair. Bud got a vest, a skateboard and a book about a famous skateboarder. The baby got a pushchair for his doll.

But the best present was in a basket by the fire. It was a puppy, a little brown sheepdog puppy from the farm.

"The farmer sent her," Mum said. "She said every brave girl needs a dog."

Then there was Christmas dinner. The table had a white tablecloth, a napkin, a fork and a knife for each person, plates, bowls, a cup each, more forks and knives, spoons and teaspoons. There was a turkey with sauce, a chicken, sausages, salami, potato and pumpkin, rice, noodles and pasta for Bud, broccoli, cauliflower, spinach, leek, corn and aubergine, a salad with lettuce, cucumber, tomato and olives, and salt, spices and herbs. There was juice, lemonade, tea and coffee. For pudding there was Christmas pudding, cupcakes, pancakes and a big bowl of fruit: an orange, a lemon, a lime, a clementine, a grapefruit, a banana, a mango, a kiwi, a peach, pears, a cherry, an apricot, grapes, a strawberry, a raspberry, a coconut and a pineapple.

In the afternoon, there were visitors. The family opposite told the children about Diwali, with a candle in every window, fireworks that go bang and pop, and sweets. The family upstairs told them about Eid, with new clothes and a big dinner with all the relatives. The family next door told them about Hanukkah, with eight candles, one for each night.

"So many families, so many festivals," Grandad smiled. "Christmas, Diwali, Eid, Hanukkah, and all the birthdays. And everybody likes treats."

After dinner they played games. Bud was Simon. "Simon says touch your nose! Simon says touch your ear, your elbow, your knee, your thumb and your toe! Simon says shoulders, neck, tummy, finger! Simon says jump! Hop! Skip! Clap! Stomp! Stretch! Bend! Bounce! Drop! Lift your arm! Dance! Sing! Now pretend to ski, skate, swim, swing, kick, throw, wave, point, sit, wait, listen, laugh, hide, read, write, knit, sweep, wash, watch, roll, push, pick, mix, make, hear, help, open, close and lock the door!" Bolts danced so fast that he fell over. "Good exercise!" Grandad laughed.

Then Grandad had a secret for them. "When I was a kid," he said, "I was scared of the dark too."

"You?" Hazel cried. "But you look after the lighthouse!"

"That is why," Grandad smiled. "I wanted to be the one who makes the light."

The man with the red hat drove to the house for Comet. He gave Hazel and Bud a ride in the sleigh, jingle, jingle, jingle.

Pebbles fell asleep in a little wooden hedgehog house in the garden, with a bed of straw and leaves. "Wake me up in the spring," he yawned. "Not before. And if it's not too much trouble... visit me."

And Mrs Mallet rode the first bus after Christmas, with its headlights on and snow on the windscreen, with a new battery in her handbag. She pushed it into Bolts and pressed the red button.

Beep. Bleep. Ping!

Bolts's eyes lit up, yellow and bright.

"Hello," he beeped. "Did it work?"

"It worked," Hazel laughed, and she gave him a big hug.

That night, before bed, Hazel looked out of her window. Far away, at the end of the bay, the lighthouse was shining.

Mum walked in to say goodnight.

"Shall I leave your lamp on?" she asked.

Hazel thought about the dark wood, and the dark sea, and the dark stairs of the lighthouse.

"No. You can turn it off."

Mum turned off the lamp. The house was quiet. Only the tick-tock of the clock, and the pitter patter of melting snow. The room was dark. But it was not scary any more.

Because Hazel knew now: the light is not only in a lamp. Sometimes, it is inside you. And sometimes, it is in your friends.

## Epilogue: Spring

In the spring, Hazel ran to wake up Pebbles. The snow was gone. There was mud and puddles and soft rain, then sun. In the garden there was a purple flower, there were daffodils, a rosebud, roses with a red petal on a green stem, blossom on the trees, and new grass began to grow in the soil. In the pond there was a frog and a froglet, a toad, a newt, a tadpole and lots of tadpoles. Ribbit, ribbit. A bee flew buzzzzz to the beehive. A butterfly and a dragonfly flew over the water. A bird, then lots of birds, began to sing: tweet, tweet. Every insect was busy. A blackbird sang on the hedge, near a web with no spider and a wasp's old nest. A crow and a pigeon watched from the roof, and a swan, then two more swans, landed on the lake.

Pebbles poked his nose out of the straw. "Is it spring?" he yawned. Near the hedge, a mole looked out of its burrow, between the roots of the tree. In the vegetable garden, there were seeds, wheat and bees. Even an old tortoise woke up in the garden next door.

"It is spring," Hazel smiled. "You can wake up now."

The End.
